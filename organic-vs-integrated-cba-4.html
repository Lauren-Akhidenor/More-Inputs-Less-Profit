Summary

Integrated farming cost 23% more to run than organic, and returned a negative ROI.

This project runs a cost–benefit analysis on organic vs integrated farming systems, using cost and yield data for two smallholder crops, amaranth and corchorus. Three metrics: net benefit, ROI, and benefit-cost ratio.

Integrated spent 23% more and yielded 16% less. Its ROI: −18.4%, against +19.9% for organic.
Research question

Do additional inputs improve profitability?

Four variables tracked: cost per system, revenue per system, net return, and downstream implications for smallholder economics.

Metrics

How the numbers were calculated

Because the dataset covers one production cycle, discounted metrics like NPV and IRR weren't appropriate here. The analysis instead relies on three standard CBA indicators.

MetricFormula
Net benefitTotal revenue − total cost
Return on investment (ROI)(Net benefit ÷ total cost) × 100
Benefit–cost ratio (BCR)Revenue ÷ total cost

The analysis holds several factors constant: a fixed market price of ₦200/kg, one production cycle, stable labour costs, average weather, no post-harvest losses, and no inflation or price volatility.

Interpretation

Integrated spent ₦9,750 more per plot and produced 40kg less.

Fertiliser and pesticide spend didn't convert into yield. Integrated cost went up 23%, output went down 16%, and revenue fell ₦8,000 short of organic. That flips ROI from +19.9% to −18.4%.

Input volume up. Output down. Profit negative.

Policy implications

Input volume is a weak proxy for profitability

Cutting unnecessary spend moved the needle more than adding inputs did. Priorities for policymakers and extension services:

  • Efficient resource allocation
  • Input optimisation
  • Cost management
  • Support for low-capital farming systems
  • Sustainable nutrient management practices

Limitations
  • Reflects a single production season. Results may vary across different weather, pest, or market conditions over multiple cycles.
  • NPV and IRR were excluded, so longer-term effects, like cumulative soil health gains under organic or long-run pesticide resistance under integrated, aren't captured.
  • The fixed ₦200/kg price for both systems doesn't account for potential organic price premiums, which could favour organic further in practice.
  • Based on a single farm/plot comparison, so it's indicative rather than statistically generalisable.
  • Post-harvest losses were excluded from both systems; differing loss rates between them would affect relative profitability.
Conclusion

Organic won on every financial metric: lower cost, higher revenue, positive ROI.

Under the conditions tested, added inputs increased cost without increasing output. For this comparison, organic is the more economically viable system.
